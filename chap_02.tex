\chapter{Project Management Methodology and Modeling}

\section{Introduction}
This chapter covers how the Riaya project was organized and how the platform was modeled. The first part presents the Kanban method: where it comes from, its principles and metrics, and how it compares with Scrum and Waterfall \cite{schwaber2020scrum, pmi2021pmbok}. It then describes the Notion board used during the internship and explains why Kanban suited this project better than Scrum. The second part models the platform: two use case diagrams for the five actors, three class diagrams of the database, two sequence diagrams for an inbound call and a callback, three activity diagrams, the deployment diagram, and the two halves of the BPMN booking process.

\section{The Kanban Method}

\subsection{Historical Context and Origins}
Kanban comes from manufacturing. Toyota used it in Japan from the late 1940s, and it was adapted to software development and IT operations in the 2000s \cite{anderson2010kanban}. The word means ``visual signal'' or ``card'' in Japanese. On Toyota's production lines, a card signalled that a part had to be replenished, which made just-in-time (JIT) production possible. David J. Anderson adapted the method to knowledge work in 2007 and added limits on work in progress (WIP) to improve the flow of work in software teams \cite{anderson2010kanban}. It is usually counted among the agile methods \cite{beck2001agile}.

\subsection{Definition and Fundamental Principles}
Kanban starts from the way a team already works and improves it step by step, without imposing roles, meetings or fixed iterations. Three principles guide the daily work:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Visualization:} The work is shown on a board divided into columns, one per stage. Each task is a card that moves from left to right as it progresses. Anyone who opens the board can see what is waiting, what is being worked on and what is finished. On Riaya this mattered because three people with different roles followed the same work: the developer, the company founder who spoke with the client, and the academic supervisor.

    \item \textbf{Limits on work in progress:} Each active column has a maximum number of cards. When the limit is reached, a new card can only start once another one has moved on. The limit reduces switching between tasks and makes bottlenecks visible \cite{anderson2010kanban}. On Riaya, the ``In Progress'' column was limited to three cards. With a single developer, a higher limit would only have meant more half-finished work.

    \item \textbf{Managing the flow:} The aim is a steady stream of finished work. The team measures how long cards take to cross the board and uses those numbers to find where work waits.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Kanban Practices and Metrics}
Kanban also asks teams to make their rules explicit (for example, what must be true before a card can move to the next column), to set up feedback loops and to improve in small steps. The usual metrics are:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Lead time:} the time from the creation of a card to its completion.
    \item \textbf{Cycle time:} the time from the start of work on a card to its completion.
    \item \textbf{Throughput:} the number of cards finished per week.
    \item \textbf{WIP:} the number of cards being worked on at a given moment.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Advantages of Kanban for the Riaya Project}
Kanban let priorities change at any time. When a new request mattered more than the planned work, its card went to the top of ``To Do'' and was picked up next, without waiting for the end of an iteration.

It also kept the time between a request and its delivery short. Each card was deployed as soon as it was merged, so the client could see a change a few days after asking for it, and a bug found during a demonstration could be fixed and deployed the same day.

The board made progress visible without meetings. The company founder and the academic supervisor could follow the work at any moment, and documentation tasks for the report went through the same columns as product work.

Finally, the board kept a record of decisions. A research card keeps its conclusion even when the idea is dropped, so the reasons behind a choice are not lost.

\subsection{Disadvantages and Limitations of the Method}
Kanban has no sprints, so nothing forces a regular deadline or a fixed moment to review the work. Any dates have to be set by the people involved.

The freedom to reorder the board at any time can also make priorities unstable. On Riaya, a new request from the client could move ahead of work that was already planned, and some cards were pushed back more than once.

Dependencies between cards are hard to see. A card is shown on its own, and the board has no field that links it to the cards it depends on. Many Riaya features built on each other, such as the callbacks on the booking flow or the plan limits on billing, and the order of that work had to be kept in mind rather than read from the board.

Without fixed iterations, it is also harder to say when a feature will be ready. The size of each card (S, M or L) gives only a rough idea, and the client's questions about dates had to be answered from experience.

Finally, with a single developer, the board is only as accurate as that developer keeps it, since nobody else moves the cards.

\subsection{Justification of the Choice of Kanban}
Kanban fit Riaya better than Scrum for five reasons.

First, there was one developer. Scrum is built around a team: a Product Owner, a Scrum Master and several developers, who meet in four fixed events every sprint \cite{schwaber2020scrum}. On Riaya, John would have been the Product Owner, and the Scrum Master and the whole development team would have been the same person. Sprint planning, daily scrums and retrospectives would have taken time without telling anyone more than the board already showed.

Second, requests arrived at irregular times. The client spoke only with John, who passed requests on as they came. A request could change a feature built a few weeks earlier, as happened three times with the plan limits. In Scrum, work outside the current sprint goal usually waits for the next sprint. In Kanban, the card goes into ``To Do'' at the right priority.

Third, much of the work was research of unknown length. Comparing speech models for Tunisian Arabic and trying different techniques took almost two weeks, and this could not be sized in advance, since nobody knew how many options would need testing. Such tasks are hard to fit into a sprint. On the board they were Spike cards that stayed open until they had a written conclusion.

Fourth, John showed each new feature to the client as soon as it was ready on the team's server. Bugs and feedback from these demonstrations had to be handled as they came, next to the planned work, and Kanban lets an urgent card go straight to the top of ``To Do''.

Finally, the board doubled as a report for the people outside development. John used it to see what the client could expect and when, and Ismail used it to follow the internship and add the documentation he needed, without separate status meetings.

\subsection{Class Diagrams: Modeling the PostgreSQL Database}\label{subsec:erd-ref}
The database runs on PostgreSQL \cite{postgresql2024}. It has more than thirty tables, so the class diagram is split into three parts: people and care, the practice, and calls with billing. Tables that belong to another part are repeated with a few of their columns, and primary keys, timestamps and secondary columns are left out.

\paragraph{People and care.} \xref{fig:erd} holds the main decision of the model: a phone number does not identify a person, since one phone can serve a whole family and one person can use several phones. A \texttt{person} is one real human, a \texttt{person\_phone} row links a person to a phone number with the relationship to the phone's owner, and a \texttt{patient} links one person to one doctor's practice. Medical files, consultations and invoices point to the patient, so each doctor keeps a separate file for the same person.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth,height=0.86\textheight,keepaspectratio]{images/diagrams/class_diagram_people_care.png}
    \caption{Class Diagram, Part 1: People, Appointments and Patient Billing}
    \label{fig:erd}
\end{figure}

An \texttt{appointment} names the person who will see the doctor and the phone that made the booking, which is where the WhatsApp messages go. When a mother books for her son, the appointment is for the son and the messages go to the mother's phone. The link to the doctor's patient file is set only when the doctor accepts the request. A phone booking also keeps the place the patient searched from, so that the next call from the same phone can offer that area again. An invoice is made of its items and its payments, and its status is computed from the payments, so the two cannot disagree.

\paragraph{Accounts and practice.} \xref{fig:erd_practice} groups the accounts and what a doctor runs. A doctor's \texttt{doctor\_application} is kept apart from the \texttt{doctor\_profile}: a user can apply several times, and the profile is created only once an administrator approves an application. The profile holds the cabinet position used by the doctor search and the weekly availability.

\begin{figure}[H]

    \caption{Class Diagram, Part 2: Accounts, Practice and Doctor AI}
    \label{fig:erd_practice}

A \texttt{secretary} links a user to one doctor. Consultation recordings and the AI assistant's conversations belong to the doctor. Usage is stored as one row per transcription or AI reply instead of a single counter, so the month's consumption is a sum and the history is kept.

\paragraph{Calls, abuse and Riaya billing.} \xref{fig:erd_calls} covers the phone side and what doctors pay Riaya. A \texttt{call} is made of \texttt{call\_event} rows (transcript lines, tool calls and errors) and can point to the appointment booked during it. A call can have one abuse report, and a \texttt{blocked\_phone} can come from a report or be set directly by an administrator.

\subsection{Sequence Diagrams: Inbound Call and Callback}
The two sequence diagrams use the boundary, control and entity notation. Boundaries are the entry points of a service (a webhook, the API, the dashboard), controls hold the logic of a call or a job, and entities are the stored data. Twilio and Gemini Live \cite{gemini2026live} are external systems. \xref{fig:sequence_call} shows an inbound call from the moment the patient dials to the moment the recording is available.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth,height=0.86\textheight,keepaspectratio]{images/diagrams/sequence_call_flow.png}
    \caption{Sequence Diagram of an Inbound Call}
    \label{fig:sequence_call}
\end{figure}

When the patient dials, Twilio calls the voice server's webhook \cite{twilio2024}, which first asks the web application whether the number is blocked. A blocked number is rejected with a busy signal and no AI starts. This check fails open: if the web application does not answer in about 1.5 seconds, the call goes on. For an allowed number, the webhook creates the call record and tells Twilio to open a media stream and start recording.

When the stream opens, the call session asks the web application for what it already knows about the caller: the name and preferred language, the people known on this phone, the place of the last phone booking from this line, and any request a doctor refused in the last 24 hours. It then opens a Gemini Live session with the assistant's instructions and tools. The instructions include the current date and time in Tunisia, so the AI understands ``tomorrow'' or ``Monday morning'', and this caller context, so it can greet a known caller by name, ask who the appointment is for, and ask whether the patient is in the same area as last time. Past bookings are not loaded up front; the AI asks for them with a tool only when the patient brings them up. The session then relays the audio in both directions for each turn of the conversation. Within a turn, Gemini Live can call a tool before it answers. When the patient names a place, it calls \texttt{searchLocation}, and the web application finds the coordinates with Google Places. Once the speciality, the place and the time are known, it calls \texttt{findAvailableSlots}, and the web application returns doctors and free slots. When the patient chooses a doctor, \texttt{bookAppointment} creates the appointment with the status \texttt{pending}, adds the 10-minute timeout job to the queue, and the session sends the new request to the doctor's calendar. The API accepts these calls only with the shared secret of the voice server. At the end, Gemini Live calls \texttt{endCall}, the session hangs up, and Twilio notifies the web application once the recording is ready, so that it can be stored in Cloudflare R2 \cite{cloudflare2024r2}.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth,height=0.86\textheight,keepaspectratio]{images/diagrams/sequence_callback.png}
    \caption{Sequence Diagram of the Refusal and Timeout Callback}
    \label{fig:sequence_callback}
\end{figure}

\xref{fig:sequence_callback} covers what happens when a pending request is not accepted. Either the doctor refuses it from the dashboard, and the booking controller cancels the appointment and adds a callback job to the queue, or the doctor does not answer within 10 minutes and the delayed job created at booking time becomes due. In both cases the callback worker of the voice server runs the job through BullMQ \cite{bullmq2024}, asks the web application for the phone number and the message, and places an outbound call through Twilio.

If the patient answers, the worker asks the booking controller for up to three other doctors with free slots, the AI offers them, and the doctor the patient chooses gets a new pending request. After a timeout, the AI asks the patient to call again instead. If the call is not answered, is busy or fails, the worker sends the same message on WhatsApp.

\subsection{Activity Diagrams: Sign-up, Urgent Booking and Answers to a Request}
The three activity diagrams use swimlanes to show who does each step. Decisions are drawn as diamonds and end states as circled dots.

\xref{fig:activity_signup} shows how a doctor joins the platform. The doctor fills in the sign-up form with an email and a password. Riaya keeps the sign-up pending and emails a 6-digit code, valid for 10 minutes, with 5 tries and a new code available after 60 seconds. If the code is wrong and a retry is allowed, the doctor types it again; otherwise Riaya sends a new code. The user account is created and signed in only after a correct code. The doctor then fills in the application (CIN photos, speciality, Medical Council number, TIN and the cabinet placed on a map), and Riaya saves it with the status \texttt{pending}. The administrator reviews it and decides. ``Approve'' creates the profile, sets the status to \texttt{verified} and unlocks the dashboard. ``Reject'' shows the reasons to the doctor, who fixes the information and reapplies, which puts the application back to \texttt{pending}. ``Ban'' ends the process.

\xref{fig:activity_urgent} shows an urgent booking. The patient describes the symptoms. For an urgent case that is not life-threatening, it offers the urgent requests directly. If a doctor accepts, that request is confirmed, the sibling requests are cancelled and the patient receives a WhatsApp confirmation. \subsection{Deployment Diagram: Docker, Nginx and the Ubuntu VPS}
\xref{fig:deployment} shows how the code goes from GitHub to the single Ubuntu server used by the development team \cite{docker2024}. The platform is not open to the public yet: the server hosts the version that the team and the client test. The modules inside each repository are described in Chapter~3 (\xref{fig:two_repos}).

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth,height=0.7\textheight,keepaspectratio]{images/diagrams/deployment.png}
    \caption{Deployment Diagram of the Riaya Platform}
    \label{fig:deployment}
\end{figure}

The left part is GitHub. The web repository holds the Next.js application and the voice repository holds the realtime server. A push to \texttt{main} starts the GitHub Actions workflow of that repository, which builds a Docker image and pushes it to the GitHub Container Registry (GHCR). The environment file and the SSH key are stored as GitHub secrets.

The workflow then connects to the Ubuntu VPS over SSH and runs three numbered steps. For the web application, step~1 runs the Drizzle migrations in a one-off container, so the database schema is up to date before the new code starts. Step~2 pulls the new image from GHCR, and step~3 restarts the services with \texttt{docker compose up -d}. Old images are then pruned.

On the VPS, all traffic from the internet (browsers and Twilio) arrives on HTTPS port 443 at Nginx, which handles TLS and works as a reverse proxy. It sends pages and API calls to the \texttt{web} container, and WebSocket connections and Twilio's call webhooks to the \texttt{voice} container. Both containers run on the Docker network \texttt{riaya\_network} with PostgreSQL and Redis, whose data lives in Docker volumes. The web application uses PostgreSQL and Redis, and the voice server uses Redis for its job queues. Outbound HTTPS calls leave the VPS for the external services: Twilio, Gemini, OpenRouter, WhatsApp, Google Places, Geoapify, Cloudflare R2 and Konnect.

    \label{fig:bpmn_booking}
\end{figure}
\end{landscape}

\xref{fig:bpmn_booking} is one pool, ``Riaya phone booking'', with four lanes: Patient, AI system, Doctor and Administrator. The process starts when the patient calls. A first exclusive gateway checks whether the number is blocked; if it is, the process ends with the call rejected as busy. Otherwise the AI greets the caller and asks for the reason, the place and the time. This task carries an escalation boundary event labelled ``Abuse''. When it fires, the AI reports the abuse and says goodbye, and the report goes to the Administrator lane. The administrator reviews the report with its recording and transcript and decides: either block the number for 7 days, 30 days or for good, or dismiss the report.

In the normal path, a gateway checks for urgent signs. If there are any, the AI advises calling the SAMU at 190 and offers urgent requests before the search. The best-fit search then offers two or three doctors. A second gateway separates the two kinds of booking: one pending request for a normal case, or up to three urgent requests in one group. The paths merge, the doctor sees the request live (with an urgent badge if needed), and a link event passes the process to part~B.

\xref{fig:bpmn_callback} has three pools. The patient and the doctor are black-box pools: only the messages they exchange with Riaya are drawn, as dashed message flows. The Riaya pool has two lanes, AI system and Server and jobs. After the link event from part~A, an event-based gateway waits for whichever of three events comes first. The ``Doctor accepts'' message leads to confirming the request, linking the patient file and sending a WhatsApp message to the patient; a timer then waits until 2 hours after the visit, and the server sends the review link. A 10-minute timer with no answer leads to cancelling the request. The ``Doctor refuses'' message leads to cancelling it and keeping the request for 24 hours in Redis.

The timeout and refusal paths merge at a gateway that checks whether the number is blocked. A blocked number gets nothing. For any other number, the AI system calls the patient back, offering three other doctors after a refusal or asking the patient to call again after a timeout. If the patient does not answer, a WhatsApp message goes out from the platform account. Part~B draws the normal request only; the rules of urgent groups, where a refusal leaves the other requests open, are in \xref{fig:activity_urgent}.

\section{Conclusion}
This chapter presented the Kanban method, compared it with Scrum and Waterfall, and explained why it suited a project with one developer, requests arriving through the company founder and frequent feedback from the client. It then modeled Riaya from several angles. The use case diagrams list what each of the five actors does. The three class diagrams explain the data model, in particular the split between person, phone and patient file, and the usage rows behind the plan limits. The sequence, activity and BPMN diagrams follow a call, the urgent flow and the answers to a request step by step, and the deployment diagram shows how the code reaches the VPS. The next chapter covers the technical architecture and the implementation.
