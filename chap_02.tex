\chapter{Project Management Methodology and Modeling}

\section{Introduction}
This chapter presents the project management methodology the Pengguin team used to develop Riaya. It covers the Kanban method: its definition, its principles, its advantages and disadvantages, and how it compares with Scrum and Waterfall \cite{schwaber2020scrum, pmi2021pmbok}. It then explains why the team chose Kanban for this project. The second part presents the modeling diagrams used to design the platform (the use case diagram, the class/ERD diagram, the sequence diagram, the activity diagram, the deployment diagram, and the global BPMN model), each with its purpose, its components, and its role in the Riaya project. Together they show how an agile methodology \cite{beck2001agile} and careful modeling let the Pengguin team deliver a complex, AI-driven platform while keeping quality, transparency, and adaptability.

\section{The Kanban Method}

\subsection{Historical Context and Origins}
Kanban is an agile project management method. It began in manufacturing, at Toyota in Japan in the late 1940s, and was adapted to software development and IT operations in the early 2000s \cite{anderson2010kanban}. ``Kanban'' means ``visual signal'' or ``card'' in Japanese. At Toyota, Kanban cards signalled when parts on the production line needed replenishing, which made just-in-time (JIT) manufacturing possible. David J. Anderson formalized the method for knowledge work in 2007 and introduced limits on Work in Progress (WIP) to improve flow in software teams \cite{anderson2010kanban}.

\subsection{Definition and Fundamental Principles}
The method rests on three principles that guide the team's daily work: visualization, WIP limits, and flow management. A set of practices builds on them to help teams deliver value continuously.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Visualization:} The workflow is shown on a board (physical or digital) divided into columns such as ``To Do'', ``In Progress'', ``Review'', and ``Done''. Each task is a card that moves from left to right as work progresses. Everyone on the team can see the state of the project at a glance, spot bottlenecks, and understand priorities without long meetings. Visualization mattered in the Riaya project because the work spanned many areas: frontend development, backend development, voice server integration, database design, DevOps, and quality assurance. Without a shared board, coordinating them would have been very difficult.

    \item \textbf{Work in Progress (WIP) Limits:} Each column has a maximum number of tasks in progress at once. The limit exposes bottlenecks and pushes the team to finish tasks before starting new ones. For example, with a WIP limit of three on ``In Progress'', the team cannot start a fourth task until one of the three is completed or moved on. This reduces context switching, improves focus, and raises the team's throughput \cite{anderson2010kanban}. In the Riaya project, WIP limits mattered most during the integration of the OpenAI Realtime API, a complex task that needed sustained concentration and could not be interrupted by other work.

    \item \textbf{Flow Management:} The goal is a smooth, continuous flow of work. Limiting WIP reduces context switching and shortens cycle time. Flow management also means measuring lead time (from task creation to completion) and cycle time (from task start to completion), which show the team where its workflow can improve. The Riaya team reviewed these metrics weekly and used them to adjust priorities and resource allocation.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Kanban Practices and Metrics}
Beyond the three principles, Kanban relies on practices such as making policies explicit, implementing feedback loops, and improving collaboratively. The most common Kanban metrics are:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Lead Time:} The total time between the creation of a task and its completion.
    \item \textbf{Cycle Time:} The time between the start of work on a task and its completion.
    \item \textbf{Throughput:} The number of tasks completed per unit of time.
    \item \textbf{WIP:} The number of tasks currently in progress.
    \item \textbf{Flow Efficiency:} The ratio between value-adding time and total lead time.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Advantages of Kanban for the Riaya Project}
Kanban brought the Riaya project several advantages:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Flexibility:} Requirements can change at any time, which matters when integrating AI and real-time communication. The OpenAI Realtime API and Twilio integrations needed constant adjustments after testing and client feedback \cite{twilio2024, openai2024realtime}, and Kanban let the team adapt quickly without fixed sprint deadlines. For example, when the AI agent's language model needed fine-tuning to understand Tunisian Arabic better, the team reprioritized tasks immediately instead of waiting for the next sprint.

    \item \textbf{Continuous Delivery:} Features ship as soon as they are ready, so the client can give feedback quickly. The Riaya client wanted to see incremental progress and test features early. For example, the WhatsApp integration was delivered and tested before the AI voice agent was fully working, so the client could validate the messaging workflow and comment on the message templates.

    \item \textbf{Transparency:} The board shows everyone the project status and priorities, which reduces the need for status meetings. In the Riaya project, both the development team and the project manager used the board, so everyone shared the same view of progress. The client had read-only access to a simplified version, which improved trust and communication.

    \item \textbf{Reduced Bottlenecks:} WIP limits expose bottlenecks early, such as the Twilio or OpenAI Realtime API integration, and with fewer tasks in progress the team can resolve blockers before they grow. When the Twilio integration ran into problems with WebSocket media streams, the team focused on that problem without distraction from other tasks, and the issue did not delay the rest of the project.

    \item \textbf{Improved Collaboration:} The board makes it visible who is working on what, so team members can offer help when needed. In the Riaya project, it helped frontend and backend developers work together, as well as the web and voice server teams. A frontend developer who needed a new API endpoint could see which backend developer had the corresponding card and coordinate directly.

    \item \textbf{Metrics and Continuous Improvement:} Kanban encourages teams to collect and analyze metrics such as lead time, cycle time, and throughput, and to base decisions on them. The Riaya team used them to deliver features faster. For example, after analyzing the cycle time of database migration tasks, the team automated more of the migration process and cut the average cycle time from three days to one.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Disadvantages and Limitations of the Method}
Kanban also has limitations that the Pengguin team had to manage:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Lack of Timeboxing:} Unlike Scrum, Kanban has no fixed iterations (sprints), so a team can lose its rhythm and miss deadlines if it is not careful. The Riaya team set internal deadlines, tracked progress with time-based metrics, and held a weekly review meeting to check that the project was moving at a satisfactory pace.

    \item \textbf{Role Ambiguity:} Kanban defines no roles such as Product Owner or Scrum Master, which can cause confusion in larger teams. The Riaya team was small and cross-functional, so this mattered less. The project manager took charge of prioritizing tasks and keeping the board up to date, and regular stand-ups prevented misunderstandings.

    \item \textbf{Difficulty in Scaling:} On very large projects, Kanban becomes hard to coordinate without an additional framework. The Riaya team was small enough to manage the board, but if the project grows in later phases, it may need a scaling framework such as Scrum of Scrums or LeSS \cite{larman2016scaling}.

    \item \textbf{Requires Discipline:} The team has to respect WIP limits and keep the board updated, or the board drifts from reality and loses its value. The Riaya team used its daily stand-ups to check both, and the project manager audited the board regularly.

    \item \textbf{Lack of Emphasis on Quality:} Kanban prescribes no quality practices such as test-driven development (TDD) or code reviews \cite{beck2002tdd}, so the Riaya team adopted them on its own. The ``Code Review'' and ``Testing'' columns enforced them: automated tests ran on every pull request, and code review was mandatory before merging.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Justification of the Choice of Kanban by the Pengguin Team}
The Pengguin team chose Kanban for Riaya for five reasons.

First, the project carries a lot of uncertainty, especially in the AI voice agent and the real-time WebSocket communication. Kanban lets the team adapt quickly to technical problems and client feedback. When the OpenAI Realtime API was first integrated, it behaved differently than expected, and the team had to rework its prompts and its tool calling strategy. Kanban absorbed this without disrupting the overall schedule.

Second, the project is built by a small cross-functional team, which benefits from the transparency and self-organization Kanban encourages. Each developer covered several areas (frontend, backend, DevOps), and the board let them coordinate without formal ceremonies.

Third, continuous delivery matches the client's wish to see incremental progress and test features early. The client tested the WhatsApp integration, the AI voice agent, and the doctor dashboard at different stages, and that feedback shaped the following iterations.

Fourth, a digital Kanban board supports remote collaboration and connects to the CI/CD pipeline. The board was accessible from any device, and the GitHub integration linked each pull request to its card, giving traceability from requirements to code.

Finally, the team had to handle urgent production issues alongside planned work. Riaya is a live platform that handles real patient calls, so unplanned work has to fit in without disrupting the schedule. With Kanban, the team could add an urgent task to the board and prioritize it at once instead of waiting for the next sprint. This mattered most in the first weeks of production, when unexpected issues needed immediate attention.

\section{Modeling Diagrams}
A system as complex as Riaya needs several complementary diagrams, each showing a different view of it \cite{omg2017uml, fowler2003uml}. The Pengguin team used six: the use case diagram, the class/ERD diagram, the sequence diagram, the activity diagram, the deployment diagram, and the BPMN diagram.

\subsection{Use Case Diagram: Actors and Their Interactions}
The use case diagram gives a high-level view of how the actors (Patient, Doctor, Administrator) interact with Riaya. It shows the main use cases, such as ``Book an appointment'', ``Manage patients'', ``Verify doctors'', and ``Monitor live calls''. It defines the functional scope of the platform and helps explain it to stakeholders without a technical background.

It has three main actors:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Patient:} The patient uses phone calls and WhatsApp. The main use cases include ``Call Riaya and speak with the booking assistant'', ``Book an appointment'', ``Request urgent booking'', ``Consult or cancel an existing AI booking'', ``Receive doctor messages on WhatsApp'', ``Rate a doctor after a visit'', and ``Be notified if a request expires''. The patient does not authenticate, because the phone number identifies them.
    
    \item \textbf{Doctor:} The doctor uses a web dashboard. The main use cases include ``Register and apply for verification'', ``Manage practice profile and availability'', ``Manage patients'', ``Manage appointments'', ``Manage medical documentation'', ``Manage patient billing'', ``Record and transcribe consultations'', ``Use the practice AI assistant'', ``Connect WhatsApp'', and ``Manage Riaya subscription''. Most of these use cases require the doctor to be authenticated and verified.
    
    \item \textbf{Administrator:} The administrator uses a dedicated back-office. The main use cases include ``Review and verify doctors'', ``Monitor the AI booking channel'', ``Operate platform configuration'', and ``View platform overview''. The administrator has the highest level of access and can manage every part of the platform.
\end{itemize}

The diagram also shows the \texttt{<<include>>} relationships between use cases, which make their dependencies explicit. Every doctor and administrator use case that needs dashboard access includes \textit{Authenticate (sign in)}, and \textit{Register and apply for verification} includes the mandatory \textit{Verify email OTP} step.

\subsection{Class Diagram / ERD: Modeling the PostgreSQL Database}
The class diagram, drawn here as an Entity-Relationship Diagram (ERD), models the database schema of Riaya. Its entities include \texttt{doctor\_profile}, \texttt{patient}, \texttt{appointment}, \texttt{invoice}, \texttt{consultation}, \texttt{whatsapp\_session}, and \texttt{subscription}. The ERD shows the relationships between them, with cardinalities (one-to-many, many-to-one) and key attributes, and it guided the design of the PostgreSQL database \cite{postgresql2024} accessed through Drizzle ORM \cite{drizzle2024}.

The main entities and their relationships are:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Doctor\_Profile:} Stores the professional information of the doctor, including first name, last name, CIN picture, speciality, Medical Council registration number, Medical Council certificate, cabinet name, TIN, cabinet city, and exact cabinet location (latitude and longitude). A doctor has one profile and can have many appointments, consultations, invoices, and WhatsApp sessions.
    
    \item \textbf{Patient:} Stores the patient's information, including first name, last name, date of birth, gender, address, preferred language, and phone number. A patient can have many appointments and consultations. The phone number is the unique identifier and is used to match the caller when they dial the Riaya number.
    
    \item \textbf{Appointment:} Stores the appointment details, including date and time, status (pending, confirmed, cancelled), reason for the visit, and emergency group ID (for urgent requests). An appointment is linked to one doctor and one patient. The emergency group ID allows multiple urgent appointments to be linked together so that they can be cancelled as a group if needed.
    
    \item \textbf{Invoice:} Stores the invoice details, including amount, status (paid, unpaid), and date. An invoice is linked to one doctor and one patient. Invoices can be sent to the patient via WhatsApp.
    
    \item \textbf{Consultation:} Stores the consultation notes, prescriptions, and medical documents. A consultation is linked to one doctor and one patient. Medical documents are stored in Cloudflare R2 \cite{cloudflare2024r2}, and the consultation record contains a reference to the stored file.
    
    \item \textbf{WhatsApp\_Session:} Stores the WhatsApp session details, including the linked phone number and session status. A WhatsApp session is linked to one doctor. The session is established when the doctor scans a QR code, and it allows the platform to send messages from the doctor's own number.
    
    \item \textbf{Subscription:} Stores the subscription details, including plan type (Free or Pro), start date, end date, and usage limits. A subscription is linked to one doctor. Usage limits are tracked to enforce the Free plan restrictions.
\end{itemize}

The ERD also includes join tables and foreign keys that enforce referential integrity. For example, the \texttt{appointment} table has foreign keys to both \texttt{doctor\_profile} and \texttt{patient}, so every appointment belongs to a valid doctor and a valid patient. Drizzle ORM defines these relationships in TypeScript, which adds type safety and reduces runtime errors.

\subsection{Sequence Diagram: Twilio Call Flow \texorpdfstring{$\rightarrow$}{→} WebSocket \texorpdfstring{$\rightarrow$}{→} OpenAI \texorpdfstring{$\rightarrow$}{→} Database}
The sequence diagram follows a patient's phone call in real time through Twilio, the Express.js voice server, the OpenAI Realtime API (via Microsoft Azure), and the PostgreSQL database. It shows how the call is received, how the WebSocket media stream opens, how the AI handles speech and calls tools against the database, and how the live transcript reaches the admin dashboard.

The sequence of events is:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item The patient dials the Riaya phone number.
    \item Twilio receives the call and sends an HTTP POST webhook to the Express.js voice server.
    \item The Express.js server looks up or creates the caller in the PostgreSQL database. If the caller is new, a patient record is created with the phone number as the unique identifier.
    \item The server responds with TwiML (Twilio Markup Language) that opens a bidirectional WebSocket media stream from Twilio to the server.
    \item The server establishes a WebSocket connection with the OpenAI Realtime API (via Microsoft Azure).
    \item The patient's speech is streamed to the AI, and the AI's voice is streamed back through Twilio onto the phone.
    \item The AI makes tool calls to the database to search for doctors, check availability, and create appointments. These tool calls are executed by the Express.js server, which queries the database and returns the results to the AI.
    \item The live transcript is sent to the admin dashboard via WebSocket, so administrators can monitor the call in real time for operations and debugging.
    \item When the patient hangs up, Twilio closes the media stream and the server marks the session as completed in the database. The server may then notify the doctor of the pending appointment via WebSocket, which starts the next step of the booking workflow.
\end{enumerate}

The diagram captures how several systems interact in real time. Developers use it to follow the data flow and each component's responsibilities, and as a reference for debugging and optimization.

\subsection{Activity Diagram: Urgent Booking Process and OTP Authentication}
The activity diagrams model two critical processes: urgent booking and OTP authentication. They show the workflow of each process and help catch problems early in the design phase.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Urgent Booking Process:} The AI detects an urgent situation, confirms with the patient, runs an ASAP best-fit search, creates up to three urgent pending appointments linked by an emergency group ID, and waits for the first doctor to accept. If no doctor accepts within 10 minutes, the group is cancelled and the patient is notified. The diagram shows the flow of activities, the decision points, and the parallel processes.
    
    \item \textbf{OTP Authentication:} The doctor registers with an email, receives an OTP, enters it, and completes the registration. The OTP is checked against the Drizzle verification table. The diagram shows the flow of activities, the decision points (for example, OTP valid or invalid), and the final state (account created or registration failed). This process ensures that only legitimate doctors can access the platform.
\end{itemize}

    \caption{Activity Diagram of the Urgent Booking Process}
    \label{fig:activity_urgent}

Figure~\ref{fig:activity_urgent} shows the urgent booking workflow. The patient calls Riaya, and the AI assistant detects the urgency and confirms it with the patient. An ASAP best-fit search runs immediately, up to three urgent pending appointments are created and linked by an emergency group identifier, and nearby doctors are notified. If the first doctor accepts within 10 minutes, the appointment is confirmed, the other requests in the group are cancelled automatically, and the patient receives a WhatsApp confirmation. Otherwise, all pending requests are cancelled and the patient is called back or notified on WhatsApp. Because urgent requests go out in parallel and the timeout always ends in a callback or a message, no patient is left without a response.

Figure~\ref{fig:activity_otp} shows how doctors authenticate with an OTP when they register. The doctor submits the registration form with an email address. The server generates a six-digit one-time password (OTP), valid for ten minutes, and emails it to the doctor, who enters it on the verification page. If the OTP is valid and has not expired, it is marked as used, a doctor account is created with the status \textit{pending administrator approval}, and a confirmation email is sent; the doctor can then log in but only reach the profile page. If the OTP is invalid or expired, the server returns an error, and the doctor can enter the OTP again or restart registration. The flow relies on two security measures: the OTP can be used only once, and access stays restricted until an administrator approves the account.

\subsection{BPMN: Global Business Process}
The BPMN (Business Process Model and Notation) diagram is a standard graphical model of the whole Riaya business process \cite{omg2011bpmn}. It covers the standard booking flow, the urgent booking flow, and timeout handling, with pools for the patient, the AI system, the doctor, and the administrator, and the message flows and sequence flows between them.

Its main elements are:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pools:} Patient, AI System, Doctor, Administrator.
    \item \textbf{Lanes:} Within the AI System pool, lanes for ``Call Handling'', ``Best-fit Search'', ``Appointment Management'', and ``Notification''.
    \item \textbf{Start Events:} Patient calls Riaya, Doctor registers, Administrator logs in.
    \item \textbf{Intermediate Events:} Appointment created, Doctor accepts, Doctor declines, Timeout.
    \item \textbf{End Events:} Appointment confirmed, Appointment cancelled, Registration approved, Registration rejected.
    \item \textbf{Sequence Flows:} The order of activities within each pool.
    \item \textbf{Message Flows:} The communication between pools (e.g., patient calls AI, AI notifies doctor, doctor accepts appointment, AI sends WhatsApp confirmation to patient).
    \item \textbf{Gateways:} Decision points such as ``Is the situation urgent?'' and ``Did the doctor accept within 10 minutes?''.
\end{itemize}

The BPMN diagram helps technical and non-technical stakeholders follow the process end to end, and the team uses it to find improvements and to check that every scenario is handled.
