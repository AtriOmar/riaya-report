\chapter{Project Management Methodology and Modeling}

\section{Introduction}
This chapter covers how the Riaya project was organized and how the platform was modeled. The first part presents the Kanban method: where it comes from, its principles and metrics, and how it compares with Scrum and Waterfall \cite{schwaber2020scrum, pmi2021pmbok}. It then describes the Notion board used during the internship and explains why Kanban suited this project better than Scrum. The second part presents the diagrams used to design the platform (the use case diagram, the class/ERD diagram, the sequence diagram, the activity diagram, the deployment diagram, and the global BPMN model), each with its purpose, its components, and its role in the Riaya project.

\section{The Kanban Method}

\subsection{Historical Context and Origins}
Kanban comes from manufacturing. Toyota used it in Japan from the late 1940s, and it was adapted to software development and IT operations in the 2000s \cite{anderson2010kanban}. The word means ``visual signal'' or ``card'' in Japanese. On Toyota's production lines, a card signalled that a part had to be replenished, which made just-in-time (JIT) production possible. David J. Anderson adapted the method to knowledge work in 2007 and added limits on work in progress (WIP) to improve the flow of work in software teams \cite{anderson2010kanban}. It is usually counted among the agile methods \cite{beck2001agile}.

\subsection{Definition and Fundamental Principles}
Kanban starts from the way a team already works and improves it step by step, without imposing roles, meetings or fixed iterations. Three principles guide the daily work:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Visualization:} The work is shown on a board divided into columns, one per stage. Each task is a card that moves from left to right as it progresses. Anyone who opens the board can see what is waiting, what is being worked on and what is finished. On Riaya this mattered because three people with different roles followed the same work: the developer, the company founder who spoke with the client, and the academic supervisor.

    \item \textbf{Limits on work in progress:} Each active column has a maximum number of cards. When the limit is reached, a new card can only start once another one has moved on. The limit reduces switching between tasks and makes bottlenecks visible \cite{anderson2010kanban}. On Riaya, the ``In Progress'' column was limited to three cards. With a single developer, a higher limit would only have meant more half-finished work.

    \item \textbf{Managing the flow:} The aim is a steady stream of finished work. The team measures how long cards take to cross the board and uses those numbers to find where work waits.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Kanban Practices and Metrics}
Kanban also asks teams to make their rules explicit (for example, what must be true before a card can move to the next column), to set up feedback loops and to improve in small steps. The usual metrics are:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Lead time:} the time from the creation of a card to its completion.
    \item \textbf{Cycle time:} the time from the start of work on a card to its completion.
    \item \textbf{Throughput:} the number of cards finished per week.
    \item \textbf{WIP:} the number of cards being worked on at a given moment.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Advantages of Kanban for the Riaya Project}
New work could start as soon as it mattered more than what was planned. After the launch, the client found the voice's Tunisian pronunciation weak. John added cards to try other speech providers (cards 098 to 106), and they went to the top of ``To Do'' the same week, without waiting for the end of an iteration.

Each card was deployed as soon as it was finished. The deployment pipeline set up in August (card 031) builds and deploys the application on every merge, so the client could see a change a few days after asking for it.

Production problems could take a slot at once. When live calls stopped working in production because an environment variable was missing from the build (card 088), the card was created, fixed and deployed on the same day.

John and Ismail could check the progress whenever they wanted, without asking for a status report. Ismail's cards for the report, such as the diagrams, the API documentation and the notes on every AI model tested, followed the same flow as product work, so their progress was visible too.

The board also keeps a record of decisions. Card 026 studied whether Riaya could show doctors' prices to patients and concluded that it should not, because the real price depends on a diagnosis that a phone call cannot make. The card stays in the Bin with that conclusion.

\subsection{Disadvantages and Limitations of the Method}
Kanban has no sprints, so nothing forces a regular deadline. John set the go-live date of 2 October and split the internship into phases, and the Phase field of each card shows which stage it belonged to.

With a single developer, the board is only accurate if that developer updates it. Moving the card was treated as part of finishing a task: a card was not done until it was in the ``Done'' column.

The WIP limit applied to ``In Progress'' only. In September and October, many cards waited in ``Code Review'' and ``Testing'' for John's review or for the client's answer, so the number of open cards grew while work in progress stayed at three.

Kanban does not prescribe any quality practice \cite{beck2002tdd}. On Riaya, the ``Code Review'' and ``Testing'' columns acted as checks, but testing was manual. Automated tests for the booking flow are still a ``To Do'' card (card 154), requested by the academic supervisor.

Finally, the board suits one developer well. A larger team would need clearer rules on who reviews what, and possibly a scaling framework \cite{larman2016scaling}.

\subsection{Justification of the Choice of Kanban}
Kanban fit Riaya better than Scrum for five reasons.

First, there was one developer. Scrum is built around a team: a Product Owner, a Scrum Master and several developers, who meet in four fixed events every sprint \cite{schwaber2020scrum}. On Riaya, John would have been the Product Owner, and the Scrum Master and the whole development team would have been the same person. Sprint planning, daily scrums and retrospectives would have taken time without telling anyone more than the board already showed.

Second, requests arrived at irregular times. The client spoke only with John, who passed requests on as they came. A request could change a feature built a few weeks earlier, as happened three times with the plan limits (cards 028, 089 and 121). In Scrum, work outside the current sprint goal usually waits for the next sprint. In Kanban, the card goes into ``To Do'' at the right priority.

Third, much of the work was research of unknown length. Comparing speech models for Tunisian Arabic (card 023) took almost three weeks, and comparing geocoding services (card 024) took about as long. Such tasks are hard to estimate for a sprint. On the board they were Spike cards that stayed open until they had a written conclusion.

Fourth, after the launch on 2 October Riaya was a live service. Bugs and client feedback had to be handled as they came, next to the planned work, and Kanban lets an urgent card go straight to the top of ``To Do''.

Finally, the board doubled as a report for the people outside development. John used it to see what the client could expect and when, and Ismail used it to follow the internship and add the documentation he needed, without separate status meetings.

\subsection{Use Case Diagram: Actors and Their Interactions}
The use case diagram gives a high-level view of how the actors (Patient, Doctor, Administrator) interact with Riaya. It shows the main use cases, such as ``Book an appointment'', ``Manage patients'', ``Verify doctors'', and ``Monitor live calls''. It defines the functional scope of the platform and helps explain it to stakeholders without a technical background.

It has three main actors. A fourth role, the secretary, acts on behalf of a doctor and is covered by the doctor's use cases with fewer permissions.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Patient:} The patient uses phone calls and WhatsApp. The main use cases include ``Call Riaya and speak with the booking assistant'', ``Book an appointment'', ``Request urgent booking'', ``Consult or cancel an existing AI booking'', ``Receive doctor messages on WhatsApp'', ``Rate a doctor after a visit'', and ``Be notified if a request expires''. The patient does not authenticate, because the phone number identifies the line they call from.
    
    \item \textbf{Doctor:} The doctor uses a web dashboard. The main use cases include ``Register and apply for verification'', ``Manage practice profile and availability'', ``Manage patients'', ``Manage appointments'', ``Manage medical documentation'', ``Manage patient billing'', ``Record and transcribe consultations'', ``Use the practice AI assistant'', ``Manage the team of secretaries'', ``Connect WhatsApp'', and ``Manage Riaya subscription''. The main use cases include ``Review and verify doctors'', ``Monitor the AI booking channel'', ``Review abuse reports and block numbers'', ``Operate platform configuration'', and ``View platform overview''. The administrator has the highest level of access and can manage every part of the platform.

\subsection{Class Diagram / ERD: Modeling the PostgreSQL Database}\label{subsec:erd-ref}
The class diagram, drawn here as an Entity-Relationship Diagram (ERD), models the database schema of Riaya. It guided the design of the PostgreSQL database \cite{postgresql2024} accessed through Drizzle ORM \cite{drizzle2024}. The most important modeling decision is that a phone number does not identify a person. One phone can serve a whole family, and one person can use several phones, so Riaya separates the person, the phone, and the doctor's patient file.

The main entities and their relationships are:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Person and Person\_Phone:} A \texttt{person} is one real human, with first and last name, date of birth, gender, address, and preferred language. It has no phone column. The \texttt{person\_phone} table links a person to a phone number (always in E.164 format) and records the relationship to the phone's owner: \texttt{self}, \texttt{child}, \texttt{parent}, \texttt{spouse}, \texttt{sibling}, or \texttt{other}. A unique index allows at most one \texttt{self} per phone.

    \item \textbf{Patient:} The link between a doctor and a person. It stores the details that belong to one doctor's file, such as the CIN and the contact number that doctor uses. Medical files, invoices, consultations, and recordings point to the patient.

    \item \textbf{Doctor\_Profile and Doctor\_Application:} The application holds what the doctor submits: names, CIN pictures, speciality, Medical Council number and certificate, cabinet name, TIN, city, and the exact cabinet location (latitude and longitude). Once an administrator approves it, a doctor profile is created with a status (\texttt{pending}, \texttt{verified}, \texttt{rejected}, or \texttt{banned}) and the weekly availability. Secretaries and their invitations (\texttt{secretary}, \texttt{secretary\_invite}) belong to a doctor's practice.

    \item \textbf{Appointment:} Stores the time slot, the status (\texttt{pending}, \texttt{confirmed}, \texttt{cancelled}), the source (\texttt{ai} or \texttt{dashboard}), an urgent flag, and the emergency group ID that links urgent requests. It references the doctor, the person the visit is for, and the contact phone that made the booking, so messages go to the right line. The doctor's patient file is linked when the doctor accepts the request.

    \item \textbf{Invoice, Invoice\_Item, and Invoice\_Payment:} An invoice has lines and a list of payment events (cash or transfer). Its status (\texttt{unpaid}, \texttt{partially\_paid}, \texttt{paid}, or \texttt{cancelled}) is derived from the payments. Amounts are stored in millimes (1 TND = 1000).

    \item \textbf{Patient\_Medical\_File and Consultation:} Consultation notes, prescriptions, and lab reports, with links to documents stored in Cloudflare R2 \cite{cloudflare2024r2}. A consultation recording and its transcript are stored in \texttt{consultation\_recording}.

    \item \textbf{Call, Call\_Event, Call\_Report, and Blocked\_Phone:} Each phone call is a \texttt{call} with a list of events (transcript sentences, tool calls, system notes, errors). A report records abuse on a call and its review by an administrator, and a blocked phone number is rejected before any AI starts.

    \item \textbf{Subscription, Billing\_Invoice, and usage tables:} A subscription holds the plan, status, and period of a doctor. Riaya's own invoices (\texttt{billing\_invoice}) are either a subscription renewal or a recording hour pack. Usage of WhatsApp sends, recording time, and AI messages is stored as event rows (\texttt{whatsapp\_usage}, \texttt{recording\_usage}, \texttt{ai\_chat\_message}) instead of one counter that gets overwritten, so the history is kept.

    \item \textbf{Review:} A review has a unique token, the rating, the waiting time, and a comment, and it is created two hours after a visit ends.
\end{itemize}

The ERD also includes foreign keys that enforce referential integrity. For example, the \texttt{appointment} table references both \texttt{doctor\_profile} and \texttt{person}, so every appointment belongs to a valid doctor and a valid person. Drizzle ORM defines these relationships in TypeScript, which adds type safety and reduces runtime errors.

\subsection{Sequence Diagram: Twilio Call Flow \texorpdfstring{$\rightarrow$}{→} WebSocket \texorpdfstring{$\rightarrow$}{→} Gemini Live \texorpdfstring{$\rightarrow$}{→} Database}
The sequence diagram follows a patient's phone call in real time through Twilio, the Express.js voice server, the web application, the Gemini Live API, and the PostgreSQL database. It shows how the call is received, how the WebSocket media stream opens, how the AI handles speech and calls tools, and how the live transcript reaches the admin dashboard.

The sequence of events is:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item The patient dials the Riaya phone number.
    \item Twilio receives the call and sends an HTTP POST webhook to the Express.js voice server.
    \item The server asks the web application whether the number is blocked. If it is, the call is rejected and no AI starts. The check fails open, so a web outage never turns patients away.
    \item The server creates the call record and finds or creates the person linked to that phone number.
    \item The server answers with TwiML (Twilio Markup Language) that opens a bidirectional WebSocket media stream from Twilio to the server, and starts a recording of the call.
    \item The server establishes a WebSocket connection with the Gemini Live API.
    \item The patient's speech is streamed to the AI, and the AI's voice is streamed back through Twilio onto the phone.
    \item The AI calls tools to search for doctors, check availability, and create appointments. The voice server runs these tools by calling the internal API of the web application, which queries the database and returns the result to the AI.
    \item The live transcript and the events of the call are sent to the admin dashboard via WebSocket, so administrators can monitor the call in real time for operations and debugging.
    \item When the booking is made, the web application notifies the doctor over WebSocket. When the patient hangs up, Twilio closes the media stream and the server marks the call as completed.
\end{enumerate}

The diagram captures how several systems interact in real time. Developers use it to follow the data flow and each component's responsibilities, and as a reference for debugging and optimization.

\subsection{Activity Diagram: Urgent Booking Process and OTP Authentication}
The activity diagrams model two critical processes: urgent booking and OTP authentication. They show the workflow of each process and help catch problems early in the design phase.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Urgent Booking Process:} The AI detects an urgent situation and, if the signs are life-threatening, first tells the patient to call the emergency number (190). It then confirms with the patient, runs an ASAP best-fit search, creates up to three urgent pending appointments linked by an emergency group ID, and waits for the first doctor to accept. If no doctor accepts within 10 minutes, the group is cancelled and the patient is notified. The diagram shows the flow of activities, the decision points, and the parallel processes.
    
    \item \textbf{OTP Authentication:} The doctor registers with an email and a password, receives an OTP, enters it, and completes the registration. The pending sign-up is kept in Better Auth's verification table until the OTP is checked. The diagram shows the flow of activities, the decision points (for example, OTP valid or invalid), and the final state (account created or registration failed). This process ensures that only legitimate doctors can access the platform.
\end{itemize}

Figure~\ref{fig:activity_otp} shows how doctors authenticate with an OTP when they register. The doctor submits the registration form with an email address. The server generates a six-digit one-time password (OTP), valid for ten minutes, and emails it to the doctor, who enters it on the verification page. The doctor has five attempts and can ask for a new code after a 60-second cooldown. If the OTP is valid and has not expired, the account is created and the doctor is signed in, but can only reach the profile page. There the doctor submits the professional application, and the other pages stay locked until an administrator approves it. If the OTP is invalid or expired, the server returns an error, and the doctor can enter the code again or restart registration. The flow relies on two security measures: the account does not exist until the OTP is verified, and access stays restricted until an administrator approves the application.

\subsection{Deployment Diagram: Docker, Nginx, Ubuntu VPS Architecture}
The deployment diagram shows the physical and logical infrastructure of Riaya \cite{docker2024}. It includes:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Two repositories:} Web app (Next.js) and Voice server (Express.js). Each repository has its own GitHub Actions CI/CD workflow.
    \item \textbf{GitHub Actions:} CI/CD pipelines that build Docker images, publish them to GHCR, and deploy to a self-hosted Ubuntu VPS.
    \item \textbf{Nginx:} Reverse proxy handling TLS and routing.
    \item \textbf{Docker containers:} PostgreSQL, Redis, web app, and voice server running on a shared Docker network.
    \item \textbf{External services:} Twilio, Gemini Live API, OpenRouter, Cloudflare R2, Konnect.network, WhatsApp (through Baileys), Geoapify.
\end{itemize}

It also shows the network connections between components, including the WebSocket connections between Twilio and the voice server and between the voice server and the Gemini Live API. The team relies on it to understand how the platform is deployed and to plan maintenance and scaling.

\subsection{BPMN: Global Business Process}
The BPMN (Business Process Model and Notation) diagram is a standard graphical model of the booking process of Riaya \cite{omg2011bpmn}. It covers the standard booking flow, the urgent booking flow, and the handling of refusals and timeouts, with one swimlane for each of the patient, the AI system, and the doctor. The administrator is not drawn, because the administrator does not take part in a booking.

Its main elements are:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Swimlanes:} Patient, AI System, Doctor.
    \item \textbf{Start Event:} The patient calls Riaya.
    \item \textbf{Gateways:} Decision points such as ``Is the number blocked?'', ``Is the situation urgent?'', and ``Did the doctor answer within 10 minutes?''.
    \item \textbf{Activities:} Collecting the information, creating one pending request or up to three urgent ones, notifying the doctor, confirming or cancelling the appointment, and calling the patient back.
    \item \textbf{End Events:} Appointment confirmed and rating link received, call rejected, or callback received after a refusal or a timeout.
\end{itemize}

The BPMN diagram helps technical and non-technical stakeholders follow the process end to end, and the team uses it to find improvements and to check that every scenario is handled.
