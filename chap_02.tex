\chapter{Project Management Methodology and Modeling}

\section{Introduction}
This chapter covers how the Riaya project was organized and how the platform was modeled. The first part presents the Kanban method: where it comes from, its principles and metrics, and how it compares with Scrum and Waterfall \cite{schwaber2020scrum, pmi2021pmbok}. It then describes the Notion board used during the internship and explains why Kanban suited this project better than Scrum. The second part models the platform: two use case diagrams for the four actors, three class diagrams of the database, two sequence diagrams for an inbound call and a callback, three activity diagrams, the deployment diagram, and the two halves of the BPMN booking process.

\section{The Kanban Method}

\subsection{Historical Context and Origins}
Kanban comes from manufacturing. Toyota used it in Japan from the late 1940s, and it was adapted to software development and IT operations in the 2000s \cite{anderson2010kanban}. The word means ``visual signal'' or ``card'' in Japanese. On Toyota's production lines, a card signalled that a part had to be replenished, which made just-in-time (JIT) production possible. David J. Anderson adapted the method to knowledge work in 2007 and added limits on work in progress (WIP) to improve the flow of work in software teams \cite{anderson2010kanban}. It is usually counted among the agile methods \cite{beck2001agile}.

\subsection{Definition and Fundamental Principles}
Kanban starts from the way a team already works and improves it step by step, without imposing roles, meetings or fixed iterations. Three principles guide the daily work:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Visualization:} The work is shown on a board divided into columns, one per stage. Each task is a card that moves from left to right as it progresses. Anyone who opens the board can see what is waiting, what is being worked on and what is finished. On Riaya this mattered because three people with different roles followed the same work: the developer, the company founder who spoke with the client, and the academic supervisor.

    \item \textbf{Limits on work in progress:} Each active column has a maximum number of cards. When the limit is reached, a new card can only start once another one has moved on. The limit reduces switching between tasks and makes bottlenecks visible \cite{anderson2010kanban}. On Riaya, the ``In Progress'' column was limited to three cards. With a single developer, a higher limit would only have meant more half-finished work.

    \item \textbf{Managing the flow:} The aim is a steady stream of finished work. The team measures how long cards take to cross the board and uses those numbers to find where work waits.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Kanban Practices and Metrics}
Kanban also asks teams to make their rules explicit (for example, what must be true before a card can move to the next column), to set up feedback loops and to improve in small steps. The usual metrics are:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Lead time:} the time from the creation of a card to its completion.
    \item \textbf{Cycle time:} the time from the start of work on a card to its completion.
    \item \textbf{Throughput:} the number of cards finished per week.
    \item \textbf{WIP:} the number of cards being worked on at a given moment.
\end{itemize}

Every card on the Riaya board stores the date it was created, started and finished, so these metrics come straight from the board. \xref{tab:kanban_metrics} gives them for the 143 cards finished between 27 April and 18 October.

\subsection{Advantages of Kanban for the Riaya Project}
New work could start as soon as it mattered more than what was planned. Around the launch, the client found the voice's Tunisian pronunciation weak. John added cards to try other speech providers (cards 098 to 106), and they went to the top of ``To Do'' the same week, without waiting for the end of an iteration.

Each card was deployed as soon as it was finished. The deployment pipeline set up in August (card 031) builds and deploys the application on every merge, so the client could see a change a few days after asking for it.

Production problems could take a slot at once. When live calls stopped working in production because an environment variable was missing from the build (card 088), the card was created, fixed and deployed on the same day.

John and Ismail could check the progress whenever they wanted, without asking for a status report. Ismail's cards for the report, such as the diagrams, the API documentation and the notes on every AI model tested, followed the same flow as product work, so their progress was visible too.

The board also keeps a record of decisions. Card 026 studied whether Riaya could show doctors' prices to patients and concluded that it should not, because the real price depends on a diagnosis that a phone call cannot make. The card stays in the Bin with that conclusion.

\subsection{Disadvantages and Limitations of the Method}
Kanban has no sprints, so nothing forces a regular deadline. John set the go-live date of 2 October and split the internship into phases, and the Phase field of each card shows which stage it belonged to.

With a single developer, the board is only accurate if that developer updates it. Moving the card was treated as part of finishing a task: a card was not done until it was in the ``Done'' column.

The WIP limit applied to ``In Progress'' only. In September and October, many cards waited in ``Code Review'' and ``Testing'' for John's review or for the client's answer, so the number of open cards grew while work in progress stayed at three.

Kanban does not prescribe any quality practice \cite{beck2002tdd}. On Riaya, the ``Code Review'' and ``Testing'' columns acted as checks, but testing was manual. Automated tests for the booking flow are still a ``To Do'' card (card 154), requested by the academic supervisor.

Finally, the board suits one developer well. A larger team would need clearer rules on who reviews what, and possibly a scaling framework \cite{larman2016scaling}.

\subsection{Justification of the Choice of Kanban}
Kanban fit Riaya better than Scrum for five reasons.

First, there was one developer. Scrum is built around a team: a Product Owner, a Scrum Master and several developers, who meet in four fixed events every sprint \cite{schwaber2020scrum}. On Riaya, John would have been the Product Owner, and the Scrum Master and the whole development team would have been the same person. Sprint planning, daily scrums and retrospectives would have taken time without telling anyone more than the board already showed.

Second, requests arrived at irregular times. The client spoke only with John, who passed requests on as they came. A request could change a feature built a few weeks earlier, as happened three times with the plan limits (cards 028, 089 and 121). In Scrum, work outside the current sprint goal usually waits for the next sprint. In Kanban, the card goes into ``To Do'' at the right priority.

Third, much of the work was research of unknown length. Comparing speech models for Tunisian Arabic (card 023) took almost three weeks, and comparing geocoding services (card 024) took about as long. Such tasks are hard to estimate for a sprint. On the board they were Spike cards that stayed open until they had a written conclusion.

Fourth, after the launch on 2 October Riaya was a live service. Bugs and client feedback had to be handled as they came, next to the planned work, and Kanban lets an urgent card go straight to the top of ``To Do''.

Finally, the board doubled as a report for the people outside development. John used it to see what the client could expect and when, and Ismail used it to follow the internship and add the documentation he needed, without separate status meetings.

The patient has no account and no password. Every administrator use case includes ``Authentication'': the administrator signs in before using the back-office, whereas the patient never signs in.

\subsection{Class Diagrams: Modeling the PostgreSQL Database}\label{subsec:erd-ref}
The database runs on PostgreSQL \cite{postgresql2024} and its schema is written in TypeScript with Drizzle ORM \cite{drizzle2024}. It has more than thirty tables, too many for one readable figure, so the class diagram is split into three parts: people and care, the practice, and calls with billing. Each part draws the tables of another part as dashed boxes that show only their identifier. The attributes shown are the ones that matter for the design; timestamps and secondary columns are left out.

\paragraph{People and care.} \xref{fig:erd} holds the most important decision of the model: a phone number does not identify a person. One phone can serve a whole family, and one person can use several phones. Riaya therefore keeps three separate tables (the orange classes in the figure). A \texttt{person} is one real human, with names, date of birth, preferred language and the source of the record (a call or a doctor). It has no phone column. A \texttt{person\_phone} row links a person to a phone number in E.164 format, and its \texttt{relationship} column says how that person relates to the owner of the phone: \texttt{self}, \texttt{child}, \texttt{parent}, \texttt{spouse}, \texttt{sibling} or \texttt{other}. A unique index allows only one \texttt{self} per phone. A \texttt{patient} is the link between one doctor and one person, with the details that belong to that doctor's file, such as the CIN and the number the doctor uses. Medical files, consultations and invoices point to the patient, so each doctor keeps a separate file for the same person.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth,height=0.86\textheight,keepaspectratio]{images/diagrams/class_diagram_people_care.png}
    \caption{Class Diagram, Part 1: People, Appointments and Patient Billing}
    \label{fig:erd}
\end{figure}

The \texttt{appointment} class carries two references that look alike and mean different things. \texttt{personId} (``is for'') names the person who will see the doctor, and \texttt{contactPhone} is the line that made the booking, where the WhatsApp confirmation and the review link are sent. When a mother books for her son, the appointment is for the son and the messages go to the mother's phone. The link to the doctor's patient file, \texttt{patientId}, is empty while the request is pending and is set when the doctor accepts it (``linked on accept''). The \texttt{status}, \texttt{source}, \texttt{urgent} and \texttt{emergencyGroupId} columns support the pending request flow and the groups of up to three urgent requests.

Patient billing follows the same idea of keeping facts and deriving the rest. An invoice is composed of its items and its payments. Each payment is a separate row with an amount, a method (cash or transfer) and a date. The invoice status (\texttt{unpaid}, \texttt{partially\_paid} or \texttt{paid}) and the paid amount are recomputed from the payment rows every time a payment is added or deleted, so they cannot drift from the payments. Only \texttt{cancelled} is set by hand. Amounts are stored in millimes (1~TND = 1000 millimes); the columns keep the suffix \texttt{Centimes} for historical reasons. A \texttt{review} belongs to one appointment and holds the single-use token of the rating link, the rating and the waiting time.

\paragraph{Accounts and practice.} \xref{fig:erd_practice} groups the accounts and what a doctor runs. The \texttt{user} table comes from Better Auth \cite{betterauth2024}, and its \texttt{accessId} gives the role level (an administrator has 3 or more). A doctor's \texttt{doctor\_application} is kept apart from the \texttt{doctor\_profile}. A user can submit several applications, each with its status and the reasons of a rejection, while the profile is created only once an administrator approves one. The profile stores the cabinet coordinates used by the best-fit search, the weekly availability as JSON, and the logo and stamp of Pro invoices. It points to one \texttt{speciality} (with English, French and Arabic names) and one city of the list of 24.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth,height=0.86\textheight,keepaspectratio]{images/diagrams/class_diagram_practice.png}
    \caption{Class Diagram, Part 2: Accounts, Practice and Doctor AI}
    \label{fig:erd_practice}
\end{figure}

A \texttt{secretary} row links a user to a doctor. A user can be the secretary of at most one doctor, which matches the rule of one practice per user. A \texttt{consultation\_recording} belongs to the doctor and may be linked to a patient. It keeps the audio link, the length, the transcript with speaker roles and the consultation note. The AI assistant stores conversations, each composed of messages. A message is a list of parts in JSON (text, tool results and proposed actions), with a status for replies still being written and its real cost in millionths of a dollar. A conversation can discuss one recording.

Usage is kept as event rows instead of a single counter that is overwritten. Each transcription writes a \texttt{recording\_usage} row with the seconds it used, and each AI reply records its own cost. The month's consumption is the sum of these rows, so the history stays available. The relation between a recording and its usage row is optional on both sides because the usage row stays when the recording is deleted: deleting a recording never gives time back.

\paragraph{Calls, abuse and Riaya billing.} \xref{fig:erd_calls} covers the phone side and what doctors pay Riaya. A \texttt{call} is identified by its Twilio call SID and stores the numbers, the direction (inbound, or outbound for a callback), the duration and the recording link. It is composed of \texttt{call\_event} rows: transcript sentences, tool calls with their arguments and results, system notes and errors. A call can point to the appointment booked during it.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth,height=0.8\textheight,keepaspectratio]{images/diagrams/class_diagram_calls_billing.png}
    \caption{Class Diagram, Part 3: Calls, Abuse Handling and Riaya Billing}
    \label{fig:erd_calls}
\end{figure}

The abuse classes are drawn in orange. A call has at most one \texttt{call\_report}, with the category and the English summary written by the AI, and a status that an administrator changes when reviewing it. \texttt{blocked\_phone} uses the phone number itself as primary key, so a number has one block at a time, and an administrator changes its expiry or removes it. The table also counts rejected attempts. A block can come from a report or be set directly by an administrator, which is why its link to \texttt{call\_report} is optional.

On the billing side, each doctor has at most one \texttt{subscription}. The effective plan is computed from it: Pro only if the plan is \texttt{pro}, the status is active and the period end is still ahead. A \texttt{billing\_invoice} is either a monthly subscription invoice or a \texttt{top\_up} for an hour pack, in which case \texttt{creditSeconds} holds the hours it grants once paid. It keeps the Konnect payment reference used by the webhook. WhatsApp sends are counted differently from recordings: \texttt{whatsapp\_usage} has one row per doctor and month, and the limit check adds to \texttt{sendCount} in a single SQL statement so two sends at the same moment cannot pass the limit together.

\subsection{Sequence Diagrams: Inbound Call and Callback}
The two sequence diagrams follow a call in time between the patient, Twilio, the voice server, Gemini Live \cite{gemini2026live}, the web application and the dashboards. \xref{fig:sequence_call} shows an inbound call from the moment the patient dials to the moment the recording is stored.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth,height=0.86\textheight,keepaspectratio]{images/diagrams/sequence_call_flow.png}
    \caption{Sequence Diagram of an Inbound Call}
    \label{fig:sequence_call}
\end{figure}

The diagram is divided into four phases: Answer, Conversation, Booking and End. The steps below follow them.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item The patient dials Riaya and Twilio sends the \texttt{/incoming-call} webhook to the voice server \cite{twilio2024}. The server first asks the web application whether the number is blocked. If it is, Twilio receives a \texttt{<Reject reason="busy"/>} answer and no AI starts. The check fails open: if the web application does not answer in about 1.5 seconds, the call goes on, so an outage never turns patients away.
    \item For an allowed number, the server asks the web application to create the call row and to find or create the person of that phone, in parallel. It then answers Twilio with TwiML that opens a media stream, and starts a two-channel recording of the call.
    \item In the conversation phase, Twilio opens the media WebSocket and the server opens a Gemini Live session. In each turn, the caller's audio goes from Twilio to the server and on to Gemini Live. A separate caller transcriber (\texttt{gemini-3.1-flash-lite}) turns the same audio into text, because Gemini Live's own transcript of the caller is very inaccurate. The AI voice comes back from Gemini Live and is played to the patient through Twilio, and the live transcript is pushed to the administrator's live call view.
    \item In the booking phase, Gemini Live calls tools such as \texttt{find\_available\_slots}. The voice server runs each tool through the internal API of the web application, which returns doctors and slots. That API rejects any request without the shared secret in its \texttt{x-internal-\allowbreak secret} header. When the patient chooses, \texttt{book\_appointment} creates a pending appointment and a 10-minute timeout job, and an \texttt{appointment\_booked} event reaches the doctor's calendar and the admin view.
    \item To end the call, the AI calls \texttt{end\_call}, the server hangs up, and it asks the web application to close the call and save its events. Twilio then sends the recording webhook to the web application, which checks Twilio's signature, downloads the audio as MP3 and uploads it to Cloudflare R2 \cite{cloudflare2024r2}.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth,height=0.86\textheight,keepaspectratio]{images/diagrams/sequence_callback.png}
    \caption{Sequence Diagram of the Refusal and Timeout Callback}
    \label{fig:sequence_callback}
\end{figure}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item When the AI booking is saved as pending, the web application adds a job to Redis through BullMQ \cite{bullmq2024}, delayed by 10 minutes.
    \item If the doctor refuses a normal request, the web application sets its status to \texttt{cancelled}, saves the refusal context in Redis for 24 hours (speciality, area, time and the doctors who already said no) and adds a refusal job that runs at once. If nobody answers, the delayed job becomes due after 10 minutes.
    \item The worker in the voice server runs the job and asks the web application to process it. On a timeout, the web application cancels the request, or the whole urgent group, and returns the phone number and the message.
    \item The voice server places an outbound call through Twilio in callback mode. If the patient answers, the media WebSocket opens, and for a refusal the server asks the web application for the refusal context. The web application reads it from Redis and returns up to three other doctors with free slots.
    \item The AI speaks first. After a refusal it offers the other doctors and books the one the patient picks. After a timeout it asks the patient to call again.
    \item If Twilio reports \texttt{no-answer}, \texttt{busy} or \texttt{failed}, the voice server sends the same message on WhatsApp from the platform account.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Activity Diagrams: Sign-up, Urgent Booking and Answers to a Request}
The three activity diagrams use swimlanes to show who does each step. Decisions are drawn as diamonds and end states as circled dots.

\xref{fig:activity_signup} shows how a doctor joins the platform. The doctor fills in the sign-up form with an email and a password. Riaya keeps the sign-up pending and emails a 6-digit code, valid for 10 minutes, with 5 tries and a new code available after 60 seconds. If the code is wrong and a retry is allowed, the doctor types it again; otherwise Riaya sends a new code. The user account is created and signed in only after a correct code. The doctor then fills in the application (CIN photos, speciality, Medical Council number, TIN and the cabinet placed on a map), and Riaya saves it with the status \texttt{pending}. The administrator reviews it and decides. ``Approve'' creates the profile, sets the status to \texttt{verified} and unlocks the dashboard. ``Reject'' shows the reasons to the doctor, who fixes the information and reapplies, which puts the application back to \texttt{pending}. ``Ban'' ends the process.

\subsection{Deployment Diagram: Docker, Nginx and the Ubuntu VPS}
\xref{fig:deployment} shows how the code goes from GitHub to the production server \cite{docker2024}. The modules inside each repository are described in Chapter~3 (\xref{fig:two_repos}).

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth,height=0.7\textheight,keepaspectratio]{images/diagrams/deployment.png}
    \caption{Deployment Diagram of the Riaya Platform}
    \label{fig:deployment}
\end{figure}

The left part is GitHub. The web repository holds the Next.js application and the voice repository holds the realtime server. A push to \texttt{main} starts the GitHub Actions workflow of that repository, which builds a Docker image and pushes it to the GitHub Container Registry (GHCR). The environment file and the SSH key are stored as GitHub secrets.

The workflow then connects to the Ubuntu VPS over SSH and runs three numbered steps. For the web application, step~1 runs the Drizzle migrations in a one-off container, so the database schema is up to date before the new code starts. Step~2 pulls the new image from GHCR, and step~3 restarts the services with \texttt{docker compose up -d}. Old images are then pruned.

On the VPS, all traffic from the internet (browsers and Twilio) arrives on HTTPS port 443 at Nginx, which handles TLS and works as a reverse proxy. It sends pages and API calls to the \texttt{web} container, and WebSocket connections and Twilio's call webhooks to the \texttt{voice} container. Both containers run on the Docker network \texttt{riaya\_network} with PostgreSQL and Redis, whose data lives in Docker volumes. The web application uses PostgreSQL and Redis, and the voice server uses Redis for its job queues. Outbound HTTPS calls leave the VPS for the external services: Twilio, Gemini, OpenRouter, WhatsApp, Google Places, Geoapify, Cloudflare R2 and Konnect.

\subsection{BPMN: The Booking Process}
Chapter~1 gives the whole booking process in one page (\xref{fig:bpmn_global}). This section shows its two halves at a readable size and explains them. Both are BPMN~2.0 models \cite{omg2011bpmn} drawn with bpmn-js \cite{bpmnjs2026}, and they are joined by a link event.

\begin{landscape}
\begin{figure}[p]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth,height=0.8\textwidth,keepaspectratio]{images/diagrams/bpmn_booking.png}
    \caption{BPMN Diagram, Part A: From the Call to the Pending Request}
    \label{fig:bpmn_booking}
\end{figure}
\end{landscape}

\xref{fig:bpmn_booking} is one pool, ``Riaya phone booking'', with four lanes: Patient, AI system, Doctor and Administrator. The process starts when the patient calls. A first exclusive gateway checks whether the number is blocked; if it is, the process ends with the call rejected as busy. Otherwise the AI greets the caller and asks for the reason, the place and the time. This task carries an escalation boundary event labelled ``Abuse''. When it fires, the AI reports the abuse and says goodbye, and the report goes to the Administrator lane. The administrator reviews the report with its recording and transcript and decides: either block the number for 7 days, 30 days or for good, or dismiss the report.

In the normal path, a gateway checks for urgent signs. If there are any, the AI advises calling the SAMU at 190 and offers urgent requests before the search. The best-fit search then offers two or three doctors. A second gateway separates the two kinds of booking: one pending request for a normal case, or up to three urgent requests in one group. The paths merge, the doctor sees the request live (with an urgent badge if needed), and a link event passes the process to part~B.

\begin{landscape}
\begin{figure}[p]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth,height=0.8\textwidth,keepaspectratio]{images/diagrams/bpmn_callback_review.png}
    \caption{BPMN Diagram, Part B: Answer, Callbacks and Review}
    \label{fig:bpmn_callback}
\end{figure}
\end{landscape}

\xref{fig:bpmn_callback} has three pools. The patient and the doctor are black-box pools: only the messages they exchange with Riaya are drawn, as dashed message flows. The Riaya pool has two lanes, AI system and Server and jobs. After the link event from part~A, an event-based gateway waits for whichever of three events comes first. The ``Doctor accepts'' message leads to confirming the request, linking the patient file and sending a WhatsApp message to the patient; a timer then waits until 2 hours after the visit, and the server sends the review link. A 10-minute timer with no answer leads to cancelling the request. The ``Doctor refuses'' message leads to cancelling it and keeping the request for 24 hours in Redis.

The timeout and refusal paths merge at a gateway that checks whether the number is blocked. A blocked number gets nothing. For any other number, the AI system calls the patient back, offering three other doctors after a refusal or asking the patient to call again after a timeout. If the patient does not answer, a WhatsApp message goes out from the platform account. Both paths end with the patient informed. Part~B draws the normal request only; the rules of urgent groups, where a refusal leaves the other requests open, are in \xref{fig:activity_urgent}.

\section{Conclusion}
This chapter presented the Kanban method, compared it with Scrum and Waterfall, and explained why it suited a project with one developer, requests arriving through the company founder and a live service after 2 October. It then modeled Riaya from several angles. The use case diagrams list what each of the four actors does. The three class diagrams explain the data model, in particular the split between person, phone and patient file, and the usage rows behind the plan limits. The sequence, activity and BPMN diagrams follow a call, the urgent flow and the answers to a request step by step, and the deployment diagram shows how the code reaches the VPS. The next chapter covers the technical architecture and the implementation.
