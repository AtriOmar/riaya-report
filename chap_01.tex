\section{Presentation of the Riaya Product (Client of Pengguin)}

\subsection{Definition and Genesis of the Project}
Riaya is an AI doctor appointment platform made for Tunisia. The project started from the observation that many patients are not comfortable with web portals or mobile applications, while almost everyone can make a phone call.

Patients book an appointment by calling a phone line and talking, without installing an application or creating an account. Doctors use a web tool to run their practice: their agenda, their patients and medical files, and their invoices. The phone line can only book doctors who keep their agenda up to date in that tool, so each side depends on the other.

\subsection{Value Proposition: The ``AI-first'' and ``Voice-first'' Concept}
``Voice-first'' means the phone call is the main channel for patients. We chose this on purpose: in Tunisia, a phone call is a familiar and trusted way to reach a cabinet. WhatsApp, already widely used in Tunisia \cite{whatsapp2024tunisia}, is the second channel, used after the call for confirmations, medical documents, invoices and rating links. Patients never need a website.

``AI-first'' means the patient side is built around an AI agent instead of menus or forms. The voice agent uses the Gemini Live API \cite{gemini2026live} with the Gemini 3.1 Flash Live preview model. It listens and answers with its own voice, so the conversation flows like a normal call and the patient can interrupt it. We picked it after comparing several realtime models and chains of separate speech-to-text, language and text-to-speech models; Chapter~3 explains that choice. During the call the agent uses tools in the Riaya system to look up places, search for doctors and free slots, book or cancel requests and save the caller's name and language. It keeps its replies short, asks one question at a time and never gives medical advice.

The doctor side uses AI as well. A doctor can record a consultation in the browser, get a transcript that separates the speakers, and ask an AI assistant about medical questions, the product or the practice's own data. The assistant can prepare an invoice or a booking decision, but nothing changes until the doctor confirms it.

\subsection{The Actors of the Platform}
The platform has five actors: the patient, the visitor, the doctor, the secretary who works on behalf of a doctor, and the administrator.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Patient:} has no account and uses only the phone and WhatsApp. The calling number identifies the line. The patient can book standard or urgent appointments for themselves or for a family member on the same phone, list or cancel pending bookings, and rate the doctor after a visit. The AI assistant handles the rest of the call, such as the language, the callbacks and the WhatsApp messages.
    \item \textbf{Visitor:} someone who has not signed up yet, usually a doctor discovering the platform. The visitor can only sign up, with an email code, and then becomes a doctor who applies for verification.
    \item \textbf{Doctor:} signs up on the website, submits professional documents and waits for an administrator's approval. Once verified, the doctor sets weekly availability, accepts or refuses phone bookings, manages patients, medical files and invoices, records consultations, uses the AI assistant, links their own WhatsApp number and manages the subscription.
    \item \textbf{Secretary:} is invited by email by a doctor on the Pro plan. A secretary handles appointments, patients, invoices and availability for that doctor, without access to medical files, recordings, the AI assistant or billing.
    \item \textbf{Administrator:} runs the platform from a back-office. The administrator verifies doctors, monitors live calls, reviews calls reported as abusive, blocks numbers, runs best-fit searches, manages specialities and users, links the platform WhatsApp account and reads platform statistics.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Business Model (Freemium: Free vs Pro)}
Riaya offers doctors two plans, Free and Pro. Both give the main practice features: patients, appointments, availability, invoices and medical files. Phone booking by the AI assistant is unlimited on both plans, so a patient is never turned away because of the plan a doctor chose. The plans differ on the features that cost Riaya money to run: WhatsApp messages started by the doctor, recording and transcription time, and the AI assistant. \xref{tab:plans} compares the two plans.

Doctors can upgrade at any time. Payments go through the Konnect.network API \cite{konnect2024}. Konnect has no built-in subscriptions, so Riaya manages the cycle itself: a daily BullMQ job \cite{bullmq2024} at 08:00 UTC finds Pro subscriptions that end within three days and creates the next invoice with a payment link. The doctor pays it from the Subscription page to keep Pro. A Pro plan that is not renewed falls back to Free.

\subsection{Plan Limits}\label{subsec:usage-limits}

The two plans set their limits for different reasons. The Free limits give a doctor enough credit to try each feature, but they are too small to run a practice on, so a doctor who wants to use these features in daily work subscribes to Pro. The Pro limits follow what each feature costs us to run.

Automatic WhatsApp messages to patients, such as confirmations and review requests, have no limit on either plan. Recording time is counted when a recording is transcribed. The AI assistant is limited by what its replies cost us and not by the number of messages, because a long chat with a transcript attached costs far more than a short question. All counters reset every month.

\subsection{Free Doctors in the Best-Fit Search}
\label{subsec:free-doctors-bestfit}

When we designed the plans, we considered leaving Free doctors out of the best-fit results, the ranked list of nearby doctors that the AI offers on the phone (Section~\ref{subsec:bestfit}), so that doctors would have one more reason to subscribe to Pro. We dropped the idea. A patient who calls Riaya should always be offered the doctor who best matches their need, place and time, whatever plan that doctor pays for. Hiding a closer doctor to sell subscriptions would make the service worse for the people it is built for. Free and Pro doctors are ranked the same way, and the plans only limit features on the doctor's side.

\subsection{Pricing Display Decision}
\label{subsec:pricing-display}

Price matters when choosing a doctor, and at first we wanted to show doctors' prices on the platform. If only the price of a first consultation mattered, this would be easy. The real cost, however, depends on many things, and two doctors with the same consultation price can charge very different amounts for what follows. We considered letting doctors enter a price for each service, which works for services with a fixed price, such as braces. In most cases, though, patients do not know what they need before a diagnosis, and an AI cannot make that diagnosis over the phone. We therefore decided not to show full prices. A partial price could mislead patients, and the platform's job is to book the visit.

\section{Functional Requirements}

\subsection{Functional Requirements of the Patient}
The patient has no account and uses only the phone and WhatsApp. The patient must be able to:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Talk to the assistant in their language:} the call can be held in Tunisian Derja, French or English.
    \item \textbf{Book an appointment:} the patient asks for a nearby doctor of the right speciality, or names a doctor directly, and chooses a free slot.
    \item \textbf{Book for a family member:} one phone can serve a whole family, so the patient can book for a relative who uses the same phone.
    \item \textbf{Send an urgent request:} in an urgent case, one request goes to several nearby doctors at once and the first one who accepts keeps it.
    \item \textbf{Manage pending bookings:} in a later call, the patient can list the bookings still waiting for a doctor and cancel them.
    \item \textbf{Rate the doctor:} after the visit, the patient receives a link to rate the doctor and leave a comment.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Functional Requirements of the Doctor}
The doctor must be able to:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Sign up and get verified:} the doctor creates an account, submits professional documents and waits for an administrator's approval.
    \item \textbf{Manage availability and appointments:} the doctor sets the weekly working hours and manages appointments on a calendar.
    \item \textbf{Answer phone bookings:} the doctor accepts or refuses each booking made over the phone, and sees what the caller said.
    \item \textbf{Manage patients and medical files:} the doctor keeps a file for each patient, with prescriptions, consultation notes and lab reports.
    \item \textbf{Create invoices:} the doctor bills a patient, records payments and sends the invoice on WhatsApp.
    \item \textbf{Record consultations:} the doctor records a consultation in the browser and gets a transcript that keeps the doctor's words apart from the patient's. Riaya then writes a structured consultation note that the doctor can edit or regenerate, and a recording attached to a patient appears in that patient's file. Recording time counts against the plan, and extra hours can be bought.
    \item \textbf{Use the AI assistant:} the doctor asks questions in a chat about medicine, about Riaya or about a recorded consultation. The assistant reads the practice's data (schedule, pending bookings, patients, free slots, invoices and statistics) and can prepare an invoice, a booking decision, a medical note, a new appointment or a new time for an existing one. Each change appears as a card, and nothing is saved until the doctor accepts it.
    \item \textbf{Link a WhatsApp number:} the doctor links a personal WhatsApp number, so that documents and invoices reach patients from that number.
    \item \textbf{Manage the subscription and the team:} the doctor chooses a plan and, on the Pro plan, invites secretaries.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Functional Requirements of the Secretary}
A secretary is invited by a doctor on the Pro plan. The secretary must be able to:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Manage the doctor's appointments:} create and move appointments on the doctor's calendar, and accept or refuse phone bookings.
    \item \textbf{Manage patients and invoices:} add patients, create invoices and record payments for that doctor.
    \item \textbf{Manage availability:} set the doctor's weekly working hours.
\end{itemize}
The secretary has no access to medical files, recordings, the AI assistant or billing.

\subsection{Functional Requirements of the Administrator}
The administrator must be able to:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Verify doctors:} review each application with its documents, then approve, reject or ban the doctor.
    \item \textbf{Monitor calls:} follow live calls with their transcript and listen to call recordings.
    \item \textbf{Handle abuse:} review calls reported as abusive and block the number for a period or for good.
    \item \textbf{Search for doctors:} run the same doctor search as the phone assistant.
    \item \textbf{Follow doctor quality:} read the patients' ratings and comments for each doctor, and stop or resume referrals to a doctor with a reason the doctor can read.
    \item \textbf{Manage the platform:} manage users, specialities and the platform WhatsApp account, and read platform statistics.
\end{itemize}

\section{Non-Functional Requirements}

\subsection{Performance and Real-Time}
The assistant must answer fast enough for a natural phone conversation, and the doctor search must finish while the caller waits on the line. New bookings must appear on the doctor's calendar without reloading the page.

\subsection{Security and Confidentiality}
Each user must only reach the data of their own role and practice, and secretaries must never see medical data. Patient data must not be kept or used for training by the AI providers. The platform must respect the Tunisian data protection law (INPDP) \cite{inpdp2004} and, where applicable, the principles of the GDPR \cite{gdpr2016}.

\subsection{Scalability and High Availability}
The platform must handle several calls at once, and the web application and the voice server must be deployable separately, so that updating one does not stop the other.

\subsection{Resilience and Fault Tolerance}
A failure of an outside service must never leave a patient without an answer, and no plan limit may block a patient's booking.

\subsection{Ergonomics and User Experience (UX/UI)}
Patients must not need to install anything or create an account. The assistant must speak briefly and ask one question at a time. The doctor dashboard must work on mobile and desktop.

\subsection{Technical and Regulatory Constraints}
The assistant must speak Tunisian Derja and never another Arabic dialect. Place search is limited to Tunisia, patients speak in Tunisia time, and amounts are in Tunisian dinars.

\section{Existing Solutions}
Before designing Riaya, we looked at the existing ways to book a medical appointment. They fall into three groups: international web and mobile platforms, local Tunisian directories, and classic Interactive Voice Response (IVR) systems \cite{voicexml2004}. For each one we noted its features, its strengths and what it leaves out for Tunisian patients.

\subsection{Solution 1: Doctolib (Classic Web/Mobile Platform)}
Doctolib \cite{doctolib2024} is one of the main European platforms for online medical appointments. It was founded in France in 2013 and is widely used in France, Germany and Italy.

\subsubsection{Features and Functionality}
For patients, Doctolib offers doctor search by speciality, location and availability, online booking, reminders by email and SMS, access to medical documents and teleconsultation. For doctors, it provides a practice management dashboard with a calendar, patient records, billing and connections to other software. In some countries it also connects to national health insurance and electronic health record systems.

\subsubsection{Strengths}
Doctolib is easy to use, has a large network of healthcare professionals and is integrated with the health systems of the countries where it operates. In France it has become the usual way to book a doctor online.

\subsubsection{Limitations and Why It Does Not Fit the Tunisian Context}
Doctolib is designed for European healthcare systems. Patients must create an account and use the website or the mobile application, which leaves out people who are not comfortable with apps or who have unreliable internet access. There is no voice assistant for patients who cannot or do not want to use the app, and no Tunisian Arabic. It does not use WhatsApp for follow-up messages, and it has no flow that sends an urgent request to several doctors at once.

\subsection{Solution 2: TunisieDocteur / Mondocteur (Local Directories)}
TunisieDocteur \cite{tunisiedoctor2024} and Mondocteur are two well-known Tunisian websites for finding a doctor. They work as directories that list doctors by speciality and city, and patients can request an appointment through them.

\subsubsection{Features and Functionality}
These platforms offer a searchable list of doctors with their speciality, address and contact details, simple filters by speciality, city and name, and appointment requests that the doctor confirms or declines. Some of them also show patient reviews.

\subsubsection{Strengths}
They know the Tunisian market and list local doctors. Patients who are comfortable with websites find them fairly easy to use.

\subsubsection{Limitations and Why They Do Not Fully Address the Need}
The patient needs internet access and, to book, an account. All interaction is text on a web page, with no voice assistant. Search relies on simple filters instead of ranking doctors by distance and the nearest free slot, and there is no way to send an urgent request to several doctors at once. Follow-up does not go through WhatsApp, and while some of these sites are available in French and Arabic, none offers a spoken conversation in Derja.

\subsection{Solution 3: Classic IVR Systems (Interactive Voice Response)}
Some hospitals and clinics handle appointment calls with a classic IVR system, an automated phone menu where the caller picks options with the keypad or short spoken commands \cite{voicexml2004}.

\subsubsection{Features and Functionality}
A classic IVR plays a menu (``press 1 for appointments, press 2 for information''), reads the keys the caller presses, and routes the call to a department or a human operator. Some systems also let the caller choose a date and time with the keypad.

\subsubsection{Strengths}
IVR systems are simple and cheap, and they handle many calls without staff. Anyone with a phone can use them, whatever their internet access or digital skills.

\subsubsection{Limitations and Why They Are Insufficient}
The caller has to go through a fixed menu, which is slow and often frustrating, and long menus lead people to hang up. The system does not understand speech, so it cannot take a description of symptoms. It does not know where the caller is, cannot find the nearest available doctor and has no urgent flow. It sends no WhatsApp follow-up, and most systems offer one or two languages at best.

\subsection{Conclusion of the Analysis}
The comparison shows a gap in the Tunisian market. Doctolib is built for European health systems and requires an account. Local directories such as TunisieDocteur and Mondocteur know the market but still need a website, an account and internet access. Classic IVR systems reach everyone with a phone but cannot understand speech or find a doctor. Riaya sits between these: it keeps the phone call that everyone already uses, and adds a voice assistant that understands Derja, ranks doctors by distance and free slot, and follows up on WhatsApp.

\section{BPMN Modeling of the Complete Project}

\subsection{Description of Business Flows (Standard, Urgent, Refusal and Timeout)}
We modeled the booking process in BPMN 2.0 \cite{omg2011bpmn}. The model has two parts, stacked in \xref{fig:bpmn_global}. Part A goes from the call to the pending request and uses four lanes: Patient, AI system, Doctor and Administrator. Part B covers what happens after the request. In part B, the patient and the doctor are drawn as closed pools that only exchange messages with Riaya, and the Riaya pool has two lanes, AI system and Server and jobs. A link event joins the end of part A to the start of part B.

\paragraph{Standard flow.} The process starts when the patient calls. A first gateway checks whether the number is blocked; if it is, the call ends at once, rejected as busy. Otherwise the AI greets the caller and asks for the reason, the place and the time. A gateway then checks for urgent signs. Without them, the AI runs the best-fit search, offers two or three doctors and creates one pending request. The doctor sees the request live on the calendar, and the flow continues in part B. There, an event-based gateway waits for whichever comes first of three events. When the message ``doctor accepts'' arrives, Riaya confirms the appointment, links it to the patient file and sends a WhatsApp confirmation. A timer then fires two hours after the visit and Riaya sends the review link, which ends the process.

\paragraph{Urgent flow.} When the urgent-signs gateway answers yes, the AI advises the patient to call the SAMU at 190 and offers urgent requests before running the same best-fit search. A second gateway then sends the urgent case to a task that creates up to three urgent requests in one group. Both branches merge before the doctor's task, where urgent requests carry a badge. The diagram keeps the urgent case at this level of detail. In the application, the first doctor who accepts keeps the booking and the other requests of the group are cancelled automatically; a refusal from one doctor only cancels that doctor's request, and a patient who cancels one urgent request cancels the whole group.

\paragraph{Refusal and timeout flow.} In part B, the two other events of the gateway lead to a cancellation. If ten minutes pass with no answer, a timer event cancels the request. If the message ``doctor refuses'' arrives, Riaya cancels the request and keeps what the patient asked for in Redis for 24 hours. Both paths merge, and a gateway checks the number again: a blocked number gets nothing. Otherwise the AI calls the patient back, offering up to three other doctors after a refusal, or asking the patient to call again after a timeout. If the patient does not answer, Riaya sends the same message on WhatsApp from the platform account. For an urgent request, a refusal sends no callback, since other doctors in the group can still accept; the figure leaves this case out to stay readable.

\paragraph{Abuse handling.} The task where the AI collects the reason, place and time carries an escalation boundary event named ``Abuse''. When the caller becomes abusive, the AI reports the call and says goodbye. The report goes to the Administrator lane, where the administrator reviews it with the recording and the transcript. A decision gateway then either blocks the number for 7 days, 30 days or for good, or dismisses the report. A blocked number is the one that the first gateway of the next call will reject.
