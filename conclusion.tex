\markboth{General Conclusion}{General Conclusion}

Riaya was developed at Pengguin, under the leadership of its founder John Jang, as a contribution to the digital transformation of healthcare in Tunisia. The project started from a concrete observation: although medical services are becoming more digital, many Tunisians still struggle to book a medical appointment because of limited internet access, low digital literacy, language barriers, or the urgency of their situation. Riaya answers this with an AI-powered, voice-first, and multilingual service that anyone can use through a phone call and WhatsApp.

\section*{Summary of the Work Completed}

The first chapter set out the context of the project. It introduced Pengguin and its founder, described Riaya and its value proposition, and analyzed the functional and non-functional requirements of the three actors: the patient, the doctor, and the administrator. It compared Riaya with three categories of existing solutions (Doctolib, TunisieDocteur / Mondocteur, and classic IVR systems) and showed that Riaya is the only one to combine voice AI, multilingual support, urgent booking, WhatsApp integration, and geolocation-based best-fit matching. A global BPMN model then described the end-to-end business process: the standard flow, the urgent flow, and the timeout handling.

The second chapter covered the project management methodology and the modeling practices of the Pengguin team. It presented Kanban, its principles (visualization, WIP limits, flow management), its advantages and limitations, and how it compares with Scrum and Waterfall. Kanban fitted a project with one developer, requests that arrived at irregular times through John Jang, research tasks of unknown length, and a live service after the launch. The board in Notion also gave John Jang and the academic supervisor a clear view of progress without any status meeting. The chapter then described the modeling diagrams used to design the platform: the use case diagram, the class/ERD diagram, the sequence diagram, the activity diagrams, the deployment diagram, and the global BPMN model.

The third chapter covered the technical architecture, implementation, and deployment of the platform. It explained the split into two independent repositories, a web application (Next.js) and a voice server (Express.js), and went through the technology stack: Next.js, TailwindCSS, and Better Auth for the web stack; PostgreSQL and Drizzle ORM for the database; Express.js, Twilio, and Gemini Live for the voice server; OpenRouter for the AI assistant of the doctors; WhatsApp Baileys, Cloudflare R2, and Konnect.network for third-party integrations; BullMQ and Redis for background jobs; and Docker, GitHub Actions, Nginx, and a self-hosted Ubuntu VPS for infrastructure and CI/CD. It also showed the main features through screenshots, discussed security, confidentiality, and access management, and described the main technical challenges (real-time latency, the best-fit algorithm, urgent requests and timeouts, usage limits, and the choice of the voice model) along with the solutions adopted.

\section*{Challenges Encountered and Lessons Learned}

The project raised both technical and organizational challenges. Technically, the hardest part was the real-time AI voice interaction, and above all Tunisian Arabic. Few models understand the dialect, and mixed sentences with French words inside Arabic were the hardest. The best models for listening and for the voice were often the slowest, and in a chain of steps the delays add up: the fastest chain we built waited about 6.8 seconds before answering. After comparing realtime and chained approaches on the same test sentences, we chose Gemini Live, which gave the most natural Tunisian dialect, a very fast response, and good understanding. Relaying audio between Twilio and the model also took careful work on WebSocket connections and audio buffering, and a separate transcriber for the caller, because the transcript that Gemini Live produces was too inaccurate.

The best-fit matching algorithm was another major challenge. It combines geocoding, Haversine distances, a 50~km radius with a far-away fallback, and a scoring function that balances proximity and preferred time. A final step re-ranks the best candidates by road distance, and an experiment on 78 searches showed that this step is a light improvement and not a game changer.

Urgent requests and timeouts relied on delayed BullMQ jobs, shared emergency group identifiers, and automatic cancellation. A patient is never refused because of a doctor's plan, and unanswered requests are cancelled automatically with a callback or a WhatsApp message. Refused requests lead to a callback that offers other doctors.

On the organizational side, I was the only developer, so Kanban's light structure suited the work. It allowed continuous delivery and quick adaptation to John Jang's requests, and the Notion board showed him and the academic supervisor where each task stood.

\section*{Future Perspectives}

First, the platform could expand to other countries in North Africa and the Maghreb by adapting its language models and local integrations. The modular architecture of the voice server makes this technically feasible.

Second, new features could serve doctors better: teleconsultation by video call, advanced analytics dashboards for Pro subscribers, or a mobile application to manage the practice on the go.

Third, the AI assistant for doctors could gain more specialized medical reasoning, while staying strictly within the limits of medical confidentiality and regulatory compliance.

Finally, the AI voice agent could keep improving through new models, better instructions, and anonymized feedback from real calls, so that it understands Tunisian Arabic dialects better and handles more complex conversations. Automated tests of the platform, still missing, are also a priority.

\section*{Personal Contribution}

This project taught me a wide range of technical and professional skills. On the technical side, I deepened my knowledge of modern web development with Next.js and TailwindCSS, real-time communication with WebSockets and the Gemini Live API, telephony with Twilio, background jobs with BullMQ, cloud-native deployment with Docker and GitHub Actions, and database design with PostgreSQL and Drizzle ORM. On the methodological side, I learned to apply Kanban on a real project, to model complex systems in UML and BPMN, and to explain technical decisions to technical and non-technical stakeholders alike.

The project also confirmed my interest in building AI products that solve concrete problems in people's daily lives. By making healthcare easier to reach for more people, Riaya shows how technology can serve the public good.
