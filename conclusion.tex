\markboth{General Conclusion}{General Conclusion}

Riaya was developed at Pengguin, under the leadership of its founder John Jang, as a contribution to the digital transformation of healthcare in Tunisia. The project started from a concrete observation: although medical services are becoming more digital, many Tunisians still struggle to book a medical appointment because of limited internet access, low digital literacy, language barriers, or the urgency of their situation. Riaya answers this with an AI-powered, voice-first, and multilingual service that anyone can use through a phone call and WhatsApp.

\section*{Summary of the Work Completed}

The first chapter set out the context of the project. It introduced Pengguin and its founder, described Riaya and its value proposition, and analyzed the functional and non-functional requirements of the three actors: the patient, the doctor, and the administrator. It compared Riaya with three categories of existing solutions (Doctolib, TunisieDocteur / Mondocteur, and classic IVR systems) and showed that Riaya is the only one to combine voice AI, multilingual support, urgent booking, WhatsApp integration, and geolocation-based best-fit matching. A global BPMN model then described the end-to-end business process: the standard flow, the urgent flow, and the timeout handling.

The second chapter covered the project management methodology and the modeling practices of the Pengguin team. It presented Kanban, its principles (visualization, WIP limits, flow management), its advantages and limitations, and how it compares with Scrum and Waterfall. The team chose Kanban for its flexibility, continuous delivery, transparency, and ability to absorb unplanned work, which an innovative and changing project needed. The chapter then described the modeling diagrams used to design the platform: the use case diagram, the class/ERD diagram, the sequence diagram, the activity diagrams, the deployment diagram, and the global BPMN model.

The third chapter covered the technical architecture, implementation, and deployment of the platform. It explained the split into two independent repositories, \texttt{riaya-web} (Next.js) and \texttt{riaya-voice} (Express.js), and went through the technology stack: Next.js, TailwindCSS, and Better Auth for the web stack; PostgreSQL and Drizzle ORM for the database; Express.js, Twilio, and the OpenAI Realtime API (via Microsoft Azure) for the voice server; WhatsApp Baileys, Cloudflare R2, and Konnect.network for third-party integrations; BullMQ and Redis for background jobs; and Docker, GitHub Actions, Nginx, and a self-hosted Ubuntu VPS for infrastructure and CI/CD. It also showed the main features through screenshots, discussed security, confidentiality, and access management, and described the main technical challenges (real-time latency, the best-fit algorithm, and the handling of urgencies and timeouts) along with the solutions adopted.

\section*{Challenges Encountered and Lessons Learned}

The project raised both technical and organizational challenges. Technically, the hardest part was the real-time AI voice interaction. Relaying audio between Twilio and the OpenAI Realtime API took careful work on WebSocket connections, audio buffering, and latency. Regional Azure deployments and a dedicated voice server did much to make the conversations smooth and natural.

The best-fit matching algorithm was another major challenge. It combines geocoding, Haversine distances, and a scoring function that balances proximity and preferred time, and it returns the top two to three doctors within a few hundred milliseconds, so the patient does not wait.

Urgent requests and timeouts relied on delayed BullMQ jobs, shared emergency group identifiers, and automatic cancellation. With this design, subscription caps never block an urgent patient, and unanswered requests are cancelled automatically with a callback or a WhatsApp message.

On the organizational side, Kanban suited a small cross-functional team building a new product. It allowed continuous delivery and quick adaptation to feedback, and it gave both the team and the client a clear view of progress.

\section*{Future Perspectives}

First, the platform could expand to other countries in North Africa and the Maghreb by adapting its language models and local integrations. The modular architecture of the voice server makes this technically feasible.

Second, new features could serve doctors better: teleconsultation by video call, advanced analytics dashboards for Pro subscribers, or a mobile application to manage the practice on the go.

Third, the AI assistant for doctors could gain more specialized medical reasoning, while staying strictly within the limits of medical confidentiality and regulatory compliance.

Finally, the AI voice agent could keep improving through iterative fine-tuning and anonymized feedback, so that it understands Tunisian Arabic dialects better and handles more complex conversations.

\section*{Personal Contribution}

This project taught me a wide range of technical and professional skills. On the technical side, I deepened my knowledge of modern web development with Next.js and TailwindCSS, real-time communication with WebSockets and the OpenAI Realtime API, cloud-native deployment with Docker and GitHub Actions, and database design with PostgreSQL and Drizzle ORM. On the methodological side, I learned to apply Kanban on a real project, to model complex systems in UML and BPMN, and to explain technical decisions to technical and non-technical stakeholders alike.

The project also confirmed my interest in building AI products that solve concrete problems in people's daily lives. By making healthcare easier to reach for more people, Riaya shows how technology can serve the public good.
